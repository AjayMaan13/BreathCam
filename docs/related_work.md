# Related work

Respiratory rate is one of the basic vital signs, yet it is still often counted by eye or estimated indirectly. Reviews of measurement technology divide the methods into those that touch the patient and those that do not. Massaroni et al. survey the contactless side (camera, radar, thermal and other sensing) [4], and Rehouma et al. review camera-based sensors specifically for quantifying respiration, a group that includes researchers from CHU Sainte-Justine [5]. On the contact side, the ECG and the pulse-oximeter waveform carry breathing information that can be extracted algorithmically, as reviewed by Charlton et al. [3]. Pimentel et al. built on that line of work and released the BIDMC dataset, whose breath annotations from two annotators on the impedance respiration signal make it a useful ground truth for testing estimators [2]; BreathCam uses it for its signal and learned-model experiments.

BreathCam's video estimator follows a common recipe: locate the torso with a pose model (BlazePose, via MediaPipe) [8], measure chest motion with dense optical flow (Farnebäck) [7], and turn the resulting one-dimensional motion trace into a rate with band-pass filtering and Welch's averaged spectrum [6]. Where this project differs from many demonstrations is in how the result is judged: it follows the measurement-comparison convention of reporting bias and limits of agreement rather than correlation alone [9], rejects windows it cannot trust instead of guessing, and keeps subjects (or patients) strictly separate between training and test sets. It also measures how much a random-window split inflates a learned model's accuracy (here, about a factor of two on BIDMC), a common source of over-optimistic results in small datasets.

For interpretation, the 1D CNN was examined with Grad-CAM [10] and the randomisation checks proposed by Adebayo et al. [11]. The outcome is a reminder of their central point: plausible-looking heatmaps need testing, and here they carried little information. Finally, because the motivating application is paediatric, the pediatric reference ranges of Fleming et al. [1] frame the main limitation: normal rates fall from about 44 breaths/min at birth to about 26 at age two, published ranges disagree with the evidence, and none of the results here, which come from healthy adult volunteers and adult ICU patients, can be transferred to children.
